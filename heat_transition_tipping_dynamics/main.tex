\title{Beyond forecasting: an empirically grounded agent-based model of how
household decisions, policy and infrastructure shape the Dutch heat
transition}

\author[a]{Naud Loomans\corref{cor1}}
\ead{n.loomans@tue.nl}
\author[a]{Floor Alkemade}
\author[a]{Lisanne Havinga}

\address[a]{Eindhoven University of Technology, Eindhoven, The Netherlands}

\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author.}

%% ==========================================================================
\begin{abstract}
%% ==========================================================================
The transition to sustainable heating in Dutch homes depends on the interplay
between the decisions of millions of owner-occupiers, landlords, and housing
associations, with infrastructure planning, and policy. We investigate how
this interplay shapes transition pathways and what an empirically grounded
bottom-up model can contribute when data is limited, and future
decision-making is inherently uncertain. Two conclusions stand out. First,
the decline of natural gas is robust, but which technologies replace it
depends on behavior, policies, and infrastructure. Second, such a model is
most useful not for forecasting a single pathway but for identifying
no-regret developments, the barriers to desired outcomes, and what it takes
to align household decisions with policy goals. Our agent-based model of the
full Dutch housing stock combines decision models for owner-occupiers,
landlords and housing associations with economic learning, social influence,
grid congestion and district-heating expansion. We run an ensemble of
plausible decision weights through scenarios, consumer segments and decision
structures. Under current rules, all-electric heat pumps are the main
alternative to gas. Hybrid heat pumps are the main fallback, whereas district
heating expands only with strong policy support. Because consumer segments
respond differently, policy can be tailored to the groups it needs to reach.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%% ==========================================================================
Energy system models and scenarios are the predominant tools used by
policymakers to plan and govern the energy transition. They strongly shape
perceptions of feasible pathways, costs and timelines, and therefore
influence investment and regulatory decisions. Most of these models, however,
determine pathways by cost optimization or stylized simulation, and are not
designed to ask how individual actors actually decide or what is realistic.
Real-world transitions deviate substantially from cost-optimal pathways
\citep{trutnevyteDoesCostOptimization2016}, because they are shaped by
heterogeneous actors, social influence, institutions and infrastructure, and
by path dependence and feedback loops that amplify early choices
\citep{barazzaKeyRoleHistoric2021,
mercureModellingInnovationMacroeconomics2019,
fouquetHistoricalEnergyTransitions2016}. Assessing what is feasible therefore
requires combining techno-economic with socio-technical and political
perspectives \citep{cherpIntegratingTechnoeconomicSociotechnical2018,
geelsBridgingAnalyticalApproaches2016}.

This gap is particularly large for demand-side transitions such as
residential heating. Here, the transition is not decided by a few large
actors but by millions of owner-occupiers, landlords and housing
associations, each deciding at their own moment, typically when a heating
system fails or a renovation is planned. These decisions depend not only on
costs, but also on attitudes, social influence, the dwelling and its
ownership, and on infrastructure. The infrastructure itself,
such as electricity grids and heat networks, is planned by other actors 
who again depend on these household decisions for utilization.
\citep{raoCriticalReviewHeat2025, hesselinkAdoptionEnergyEfficient2019}.

Socio-technical energy transition (STET) models have been proposed to bridge
this gap, by combining techno-economic detail with explicit representations
of actors, behavior and institutions
\citep{liReviewSociotechnicalEnergy2015a,hirtReviewLinkingModels2020,
trutnevyteSocietalTransformationsModels2019}. Agent-based models in
particular have been used to simulate the adoption of energy technologies
from individual decisions \citep{raiAgentbasedModelingEnergy2015,
kieslingAgentbasedSimulationInnovation2012,
mogliaReviewAgentBasedModelling2017}. Many of these models are stylized or
local. Those that are empirically grounded face a fundamental data problem,
the behavioral parameters that drive the simulated dynamics cannot be
identified from the short and noisy adoption records that are available
\citep{holtzProspectsModellingSocietal2015,mcdowallTenChallengesComputer2017,
kohlerModellingSustainabilityTransitions2018}.

The contribution of this paper lies in bridging these two gaps. We develop an
empirically grounded agent-based model of the Dutch residential heat
transition and use it to explore transition pathways.
First, whereas most STET models are stylized or local, ours covers the full
Dutch housing stock with techno-economic detail per dwelling, heterogeneous
actors and feedback loops. It combines a theory-of-planned-behavior decision
model for owner-occupiers \citep{okurAdoptionRenewableHeating2024} with
cost-based decisions for landlords and housing associations, and represents
economic learning, social influence, grid congestion and the expansion of
district-heating networks, so that its pathways can be compared with national
transition scenarios. 
Second, whereas empirically grounded models are usually
calibrated to a single parameterization, we keep the empirical grounding
while exploring the uncertainty that calibration cannot reduce. Only two
years of consistent adoption data are available, from which calibration 
cannot pin down an decisive set of decision weights. Rather than
choosing one, we retain this ensemble of plausible weight sets and carry it
through a broad set of scenarios, consumer segments, sensitivity analyses and
an alternative decision structure.

We address two questions. First, which features of the transition pathway are
robust across plausible behavior, and which depend on behavior, policy and
infrastructure? Second, what can an empirically grounded bottom-up model
contribute to transition planning when data are limited and future
decision-making is inherently uncertain? The Dutch case suits these
questions. More than eight in ten homes still use natural gas, and several
low-carbon alternatives compete, each relying on different infrastructure.

Section~\ref{sec:context} introduces the Dutch residential heat transition
and Section~\ref{sec:dynamics} the transition dynamics represented in the
model. Section~\ref{method} describes the model, Section~\ref{sec:design} the
analysis design and Section~\ref{sec:calibrating} the calibration.
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the results, which are discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}; Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Residential heat transition in the Netherlands}
\label{sec:context}
%% ==========================================================================

The residential heat transition in the Netherlands is a timely case, as it
stands at the threshold of a new phase that requires decisive action in the
coming years. The current heating regime is based on natural gas. In 2025,
76\,\% of homes had an individual gas boiler and another 5\,\% collective gas
heating \citep{statistiekHoofdverwarmingsinstallatiesWoningen20252026}. This
is the most gas-dominated residential heating regime in Europe. For
reference, the United Kingdom, Germany, Belgium and Italy also have
significant shares of natural gas based heating, while the highest shares of
district heating are found in Scandinavian and Eastern European countries
\citep{bertelsenEU28ResidentialHeat2020}.

Three main alternatives compete to replace the gas boiler: the hybrid heat
pump, the all-electric heat pump and district heating. Where each alternative
has its own dynamics, and prefered solutions differ per stakeholder,
dwelling, and local context
\citep{loomansExploringTradeoffsDecisionsupport2024}. Owner-occupiers have
rapidly adopted (hybrid) heat pumps in recent years, but this pathway is now
entering a bottleneck due to grid congestion. Studies point to lower societal
costs when more district heating is part of the mix
\citep{ActualisatieStartanalyseAardgasvrije,
pblStartanalyseAardgasvrijeBuurten2020}, but for individual consumers its
costs are high \citep{saliteAssessingAdoptionSustainable2024}. The choice is
made by owner-occupiers, private landlords, housing associations and owners'
associations, while municipalities, grid operators and district-heating
operators plan the infrastructure on which each alternative depends.

The Dutch transition scenarios and policy plans show how open the route still
is. Depending on the scenario of the Dutch grid operators, hybrid heat pumps
heat between 10 and 60\,\% of homes in 2050, electric heat pumps between 25
and 55\,\% and district heating between 10 and 30\,\%
(Figure~\ref{fig:scenarios},
\citealp{netbeheernederlandScenariosEditie2025}). Municipal heat plans
reflect this uncertainty, with about 20\,\% of dwellings planned on district
heating, and 65\,\% with several options kept open or no plan yet
\citep{molenOverzichtTransitievisiesWarmte2023}. These differences reflect
not only the uncertainty about the pathway, but also the different roles of
these organizations. Households are adopting heat pumps, in line with the
current regime of individual solutions, whereas municipalities have more
influence on investments in district heating and see it as a way to reach
their climate goals with less dependence on individual household decisions.
Which pathway materializes depends on the decisions of millions of owners and
their interactions, which aggregate scenario models represent only
implicitly.

\section{Transition dynamics in the heat transition}
\label{sec:dynamics}
%% ==========================================================================

Socio-technical transitions emerge from the interplay of many actors,
technologies, institutions, and infrastructures
\citep{geelsTechnologicalTransitionsEvolutionary2002,
geelsTechnologicalTransitionsSystem2005}. The literature describes a wide
range of dynamics in this interplay, among them the S-shaped diffusion of
innovations \citep{rogersDiffusionInnovations2003,bassNewProductGrowth1969},
threshold models of technological transitions
\citep{zeppiniThresholdsModelsTechnological2014}, interactions between niches
and incumbent regimes \citep{geelsTypologySociotechnicalTransition2007},
feedback between policy, technology, firms, users and the wider public
\citep{geelsSociotechnicalTransitionPerspective2023}, and cascades across
systems \citep{geelsCrosssystemCascadesDrivers2026}. A model of a specific
transition cannot represent all of them, and should focus on those most
relevant to the case. The socio-technical energy transition (STET)
perspective characterizes such models by techno-economic detail, explicit
actor heterogeneity and transition pathway dynamics
\citep{liReviewSociotechnicalEnergy2015a}. Based on the characteristics of
the Dutch heat transition, we translate these into three dynamics:
actor heterogeneity in decision-making, feedback loops in economic and social
learning, and dependencies on infrastructure shaped by economies of scale.
Techno-economic detail enters all three, through the costs for actors, the
learning curves of the technologies, and the costs and developments of
networks. These dynamics are typical of transitions in which many actors make
long-term investments that depend on shared infrastructure, and are usually
simplified in energy models and scenarios.

\paragraph{Heterogeneous decisions and decision rules.}
Adoption models of sustainable technologies typically combine economic,
psychological and social factors rather than a single threshold such as cost
parity \citep{raoCriticalReviewHeat2025}. In the heat transition, these
factors differ not only between households, but also in how decisions are
made. Owner-occupiers weigh costs against a range of psychological, social,
financial and practical factors \citep{jagerHouseholdHeatPump2025}. Landlords
decide mainly on costs, but face a split incentive, as tenants usually pay
the energy bill \citep{aydinSplitIncentivesEnergy2026}. Housing associations
invest mostly in steps, as part of planned maintenance, guided by sectoral
targets and by performance agreements with their municipality, and are key
partners in the neighborhood-oriented expansion of heat networks
\citep{vanderbentFutureSustainableHousing2022}. Lastly, in apartment blocks
with collective heating,  owners have to decide collectively through the
owners' association. 
Furthermore, from a techno-economic perspective, the suitable options
differ per dwelling type, installed system, and insulation level. These
differences in groups and consumer segments means that niches are created,
for whom different heating solutions are most suited. These niches can serve
as incubators where learning and feedback loops can be triggered and increase
the competitive advantage of one solution over another.

\paragraph{Reinforcing feedback loops.}
Two reinforcing loops connect individual decisions over time. In economic
learning, every installed unit lowers the cost of the next through
learning-by-doing 
\citep{arthurCompetingTechnologiesIncreasing1989}. Here, the alternatives
differ \citep{loomansExploringTradeoffsDecisionsupport2024}. Heat pumps are a
granular technology that households adopt at their own moment, and granular
technologies tend to learn fast
\citep{wilsonGranularTechnologiesAccelerate2020}. District heating relies on
large projects with economies of scale, which are prone to delays and cost
overruns \citep{flyvbjergHowBigThings2023}. In social learning, households
are influenced by what their peers do, as in threshold and network models of
diffusion \citep{granovetterThresholdModelsCollective1978,
wattsSimpleModelGlobal2002,centolaComplexContagionsWeakness2007}. Such loops
can produce non-linear, self-reinforcing adoption and, when all parts of the
system align and thresholds are crossed, the tipping dynamics described in
the transitions literature
\citep{geelsSociotechnicalTransitionPerspective2023,
lentonOperationalisingPositiveTipping2022/ed}. Whether they accelerate an
alternative or strengthen the incumbent, however, depends on the state of the
system. Social learning that follows the prevailing norm reinforces the
incumbent, whereas a committed minority in closely connected groups can
overturn an established convention once it reaches a critical mass
\citep{centolaComplexContagionsWeakness2007,
centolaExperimentalEvidenceTipping2018}.

\paragraph{Infrastructure, policy and coordination.}
Transitions are shaped by the inertia of existing infrastructure, rules and
institutions \citep{geelsTechnologicalTransitionsSystem2005,
verbongOngoingEnergyTransition2007}. In the heat transition, every option
depends on a network: the electricity grid, the gas grid or a heat network.
Because the costs of these networks are largely fixed, the cost per household
falls as more households connect in an area, and rises for those who remain
when others leave. These increasing returns create path dependence and
lock-in \citep{arthurCompetingTechnologiesIncreasing1989,
unruhUnderstandingCarbonLockin2000}, and make a single system per area the
cheapest. This, however, conflicts with freedom of choice, and requires rapid
action and strong coordination, as otherwise owner-occupiers invest in
whichever solution fits their dwelling and preferences best. The networks are
planned by other actors than the households who use them, and how their costs
are passed on to users is a policy choice. Housing associations can act as
launching customers for heat networks
\citep{vanderbentFutureSustainableHousing2022}, whereas without a connection
obligation, district heating carries a demand risk when individual
alternatives are preferred \citep{herrerasmartinezWhyGoPublic2023}. In the
Netherlands, municipalities take a central, orchestrating role in this
coordination \citep{herrerasmartinezMunicipalitiesKeyActors2022}. The heat
transition is also coupled to other systems, as electrified heating and
mobility meet on the same electricity grid
\citep{loomansExploringTradeoffsDecisionsupport2024,
rinaldiDecarbonisingHeatOptimal2021}.

These dynamics are also where the main uncertainty lies. Costs and technical
solutions are relatively well documented, but how actors weigh them, how
strongly social learning acts, and how infrastructure and policy develop are
not. Because the dynamics interact and reinforce themselves, differences in
them can lead to substantially different pathways. Transition models are
therefore better used to explore the range of pathways that plausible
dynamics produce than to predict one
\citep{holtzProspectsModellingSocietal2015,
moallemiModellingTransitionsVirtues2019,walkerDeepUncertainty2013}. This
exploration is the core of our analysis. Table~\ref{tab:dynamics} structures
the remainder of this paper by summarizing, for each dynamic, the
characteristic of the case that makes it important, how it is represented in
the model, how its uncertainty is treated, and where its effects are analyzed
in the results.

\section{Modelling transition dynamics}
\label{method}
%% ==========================================================================

We capture the dynamics of Table~\ref{tab:dynamics} with an agent-based
model. A method suited to heterogeneous actors who influence each other and
their environment, and widely used to model energy transitions
\citep{hansenAgentbasedModellingSociotechnical2019b,
liReviewSociotechnicalEnergy2015a}, technology adoption
\citep{mogliaReviewAgentBasedModelling2017,
duModellingEnergyefficientRenovation2022} and infrastructure decisions
\citep{chappinAgentbasedModellingEnergy2010}. Compared to the adoption of
electric vehicles or solar panels, the residential heat transition has
received less attention \citep{hansenAgentbasedModellingSociotechnical2019b}
and differs in two ways. Households choose between several competing
alternatives rather than making a yes-or-no decision, a competition often
missing in adoption models \citep{hesselinkAdoptionEnergyEfficient2019}.
Second, the choice depends strongly on the dwelling and on contextual factors
such as the construction of a heat network. Existing models mostly address
heat pumps and insulation by individual owner-occupiers
\citep{raoCriticalReviewHeat2025}, with notable exceptions for owner
associations and thermal energy communities
\citep{navaguerreroAgentBasedModelingThermal2019,
fouladvandFormationContinuationThermal2020} and district heating
\citep{buschScalingLocalEnergy2017,akhatovaAgentBasedModellingUrban2022}. Our
model combines these elements for the full housing stock.

\subsection{Model description}

The model simulates every dwelling in the Netherlands in annual time steps.
Each year, the owners of dwellings whose heating system reaches the end of
its life, or who face an opportunity to replace it, decide on their heating
method. Afterwards the district-heating operator and the grid operator
respond (Figure~\ref{fig:flowchart}, Table~\ref{tab:agents}). Neighborhoods
are the spatial unit at which networks are planned and grid capacity is
assessed. With these actors, all above-mentioned dynamics can be modelled.
Heterogeneous dwelling owners with a high level of detail on ownership and
dwelling data based on public databases, economic learning based on
cumulative installed capacity per heating method, social learning through
social networks, infrastructural detail through the electricity and heat grid
operators, and policy measures by means of scenarios. Policy enters through
the scenarios, as rules, regulations and subsidies, rather than as an
endogenous feedback, because quantitative evidence on how Dutch heat policy
responds to adoption is lacking. The model is documented in full following
the ODD protocol in \ref{app:model}.

Each of the actors is further described in the following section.

\subsubsection{Dwellings and costs}

Each dwelling has its construction year, size and type from the Dutch
building register \citep{kadasterDatasetBasisregistratieAdressen2022}, and a
heating method, in-house heat distribution system and insulation level drawn
from neighborhood statistics. Heating options are compared by their
equivalent annual cost (EAC), which puts options with different investments,
running costs and lifetimes on an equal footing
\citep{hastingsFinancialMethods2021}. The EAC includes the insulation and
low-temperature in-house heat distribution that an electric heat pump requires. 
These raise
the investment but lower the heat demand. Heat demand, electricity demand and
insulation costs per dwelling type, construction year and energy label are
taken from the Vesta MAIS model \citep{pblHetVestaMAIS2017,
vandermolenFunctioneelOntwerpVesta2021}. Independently of heating decisions,
dwellings are renovated on average once every 25 years, which improves
insulation and heat distribution at random and so changes the cost comparison
over time. After all owners have decided, the investment costs of each
technology fall with its cumulative installations along a learning curve
(Wright's law). Energy prices follow the Dutch Climate and Energy Outlook
\citep{pbl_kev_2025} translated into consumer tariffs including taxes,
district heat is priced at the regulated tariffs for both energy and
connection costs \citep{marktTarievenWarmteKoude}, and investment subsidies
follow the Dutch schemes. These assumptions, the spread of equipment
lifetimes, and the exogenous renovation of the stock are detailed in
\ref{app:cost}.

\subsubsection{Owner-occupiers}

The 4.6 million owner-occupied dwellings (57\,\% of the stock) are each
represented by a household that decides on its heating method. Household
adoption models of sustainable heating are varied. The theory of planned
behavior (TPB) is the most used framework
\citep{sophaExploringPolicyOptions2011,sophaAdoptionDiffusionHeating2013,
okurAdoptionRenewableHeating2024}, but many models use a utility function
without a theoretical basis \citep{raoCriticalReviewHeat2025}. Commonly
included factors are economic utility, in the form of investment costs,
discounted annual costs or payback times
\citep[e.g.][]{snapeWillDomesticConsumers2015,melesAdoptionRenewableHome2022,
michelsenMotivationalFactorsInfluencing2013}, often combined with detailed
modelling of a heterogeneous building stock
\citep[e.g.][]{leeAnalysisUKPolicies2014}; attitudes towards sustainability,
ease of use or novelty \citep[e.g.][]{snapeWillDomesticConsumers2015,
melesAdoptionRenewableHome2022}; and social influence, through the number of
adopters in a network or through explicit peer networks
\citep[e.g.][]{snapeWillDomesticConsumers2015,
navaguerreroAgentBasedModelingThermal2019,melesAdoptionRenewableHome2022}. A
review of agent-based models of household energy decisions shows that models
of investment decisions grounded in a behavioral analysis include more social
determinants, in line with the role of peers in such decisions, but that
almost half of the models do not report why they include the determinants
they do \citep{rietkerkBridgingBehavioralTheory2025}. We therefore take the
determinants from an empirical study of
Dutch owner-occupiers' heating choices
\citep{okurAdoptionRenewableHeating2024}.

Models also differ in the structure of the decision. Most utility-based
models use a single stage, in which a household adopts once a threshold is
reached \citep[e.g.][]{snapeWillDomesticConsumers2015,
melesAdoptionRenewableHome2022}. Multi-stage models acknowledge that heating
decisions are complex \citep{ebrahimigharehbaghiUnravellingDutchHomeowners2019}. 
\citet{hecherTriggerMattersDecisionmaking2017}
distinguish a trigger, a selection and a decision stage, with triggers that
are problem-based, opportunity-based or a new building. A problem-based
trigger, when the current system fails, mostly leads to like-for-like
replacement, whereas an opportunity, such as a subsidy program or a planned
renovation, more often leads to a switch
\citep{broersDecidedDividedEmpirical2019}. Because heating systems are
replaced only at such moments, the pace of the transition is bounded by their
lifetime, which creates inertia in the incumbent regime. Even when an
alternative becomes attractive, it is adopted only as existing systems are
replaced. We therefore trigger decisions both at the end of a system's life
and at opportunities before it, after which the household selects the option
with the highest utility. For this utility we use a relatively simple TPB
function following \citet{okurAdoptionRenewableHeating2024}, as the focus of
this paper is on the interactions between actors rather than on the detail of
the individual decision.

The attitude of a household towards each heating method is fixed at
initialization. 
The subjective norm reflects social influence. Its basis is the share
of peers in the household's network using a method. This share is amplified
by the method's salience $\sigma_{hm}$ (Equation~\ref{eq:sn}), which can be 
seen as its visibility or a per-method measure of how much attention the 
method draws. It combines two
sources of visibility (Equation~\ref{eq:salience}): novelty, which makes a
method stand out while it is still rare, and prevalence, which makes a
common method the norm. The faster a method's cumulative installations
grow, the more weight novelty receives, otherwise salience follows
prevalence. Adoption of an emerging, rapidly spreading method by peers
therefore weighs more than that of an established one, but only through the
household's own network. The strength of this channel is scaled by a
social-learning rate that is varied in the scenarios.
Perceived behavioral
control (PBC) combines affordability, one minus the EAC normalized on a
logarithmic scale across all options of the year, and the effort of switching
(Equation~\ref{eq:pbc}). Attitude, subjective norm and PBC combine into an
intention (Equation~\ref{eq:intention}), and intention and PBC into a utility
(Equation~\ref{eq:utility}). Of the options available to the dwelling (for
instance, district heating only where a network exists), the household
chooses the one with the highest utility plus a random term, which represents
everything the utility does not capture (Equation~\ref{eq:argmax}). Here
$\varepsilon_{hm}$ is an independent standard Gumbel draw per option and
decision, and $\lambda$ its scale.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:sn}
  SN_{hm} = \min\!\left(1,\; s_{hm}\cdot\left(1 + \sigma_{hm}\right)\right)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:pbc}
  PBC_{hm} = \frac{\left(1 - {EAC}_{hm,norm}\right)\cdot w_{costs}
    + \left(1 - Effort_{hm}\right)\cdot w_{effort}}
    {w_{costs} + w_{effort}}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:intention}
  Intention_{hm} = \frac{Att_{hm}\cdot w_{Att}
    + SN_{hm,n}\cdot w_{SN}
    + PBC_{hm,n}\cdot w_{PBC, int}}
    {w_{Att} + w_{SN} + w_{PBC ,int}}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:utility}
  Utility_{hm} = \frac{Intention_{hm}\cdot w_{intention}
    + PBC_{hm}\cdot w_{PBC, util}}
    {w_{intention} + w_{PBC, util}}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:argmax}
  \text{Selected heating method} = \argmax_{hm \in HM} \left( Utility_{hm} +
  \lambda\,\varepsilon_{hm} \right)
\end{equation}

Attitudes are drawn from distributions
based on \citet{okurAdoptionRenewableHeating2024} and
\citet{broersDecidedDividedEmpirical2019}, with correlated parameters drawn
by Cholesky decomposition \citep{edlinCorrelatedParametersCholesky2015}, and
each household is connected to a small-world peer network in which it links
to owner-occupiers with a similar attitude in its municipality, similar
to the method used by \citet{loomansQuantifyingPotentialEnergy2026}.

\subsubsection{Other actors}

\emph{Housing associations and landlords} own 29 and 15\,\% of the
stock. Their actual decisions involve tenants, local governments and, for
private landlords, a wide variety of motives
\citep{filippidouAreWeMoving2017,hoppeAdoptionInnovativeEnergy2012,
lennartzParticuliereVerhuurdersOp2019,
marzEconomicsUnderstandingDecisionmaking2018}. As data to model this are
lacking, both are represented as cost-minimizing actors that choose the
option with the lowest EAC when a system needs replacement. Although tenants
usually pay the energy bill, we use the full EAC, as lower energy costs
can be reflected in rents
\citep{chegutEnergyEfficiencyEconomic2016}. Housing associations decide for
all their dwellings in a block at once, and can alternatively follow the
municipal heat plans \citep{molenOverzichtTransitievisiesWarmte2023}, in
which they can act as launching customers for heat networks.

\emph{The district-heating operator} builds heat networks per
neighborhood, either following municipal plans or where the heat demand of
the neighborhood's dwellings exceeds a break-even density of 600\,GJ/ha. A
network becomes operational after a construction time of five years or for
density-based networks the completion year is drawn at random up to 2045, as
not all networks can be built at once.  
Once a network exists, all dwellings in the neighborhood can
connect. In practice, public acceptance, policy
support and business models are also essential to such expansion
\citep{bertelsenImplementingLargescaleHeating2021,
saliteAssessingAdoptionSustainable2024}.

\emph{The grid operator} computes the maximum electricity demand per
neighborhood after all adoption decisions, including that of electric
vehicles, which follow an S-shaped adoption curve, and reinforces the grid
where the capacity is exceeded. Until then, the grid can constrain the
adoption of electric heat pumps depending on scenario assumptions.

\section{Analysis design}
\label{sec:design}
%% ==========================================================================
The dynamics of Section~\ref{sec:dynamics} are hard to calibrate and
validate. Data on them are scarce, and fitting the model to current adoption
would extrapolate current trends, while we are interested precisely in the
deviations from those trends. We therefore use an exploratory design that
treats each dynamic according to what can be known about it
(Table~\ref{tab:dynamics}).

The decisions of owner-occupiers are governed by the decision weights in
Equations~\ref{eq:pbc}--\ref{eq:utility}. How strongly attitude, peers,
affordability, and effort count in the choice of a heating method. 
The weights determine current day adoption and can therefore be 
estimated using calibration. We calibrate them together with the
strength of the random term in the choice, against observed adoption from 2022-2024.
As the short data series cannot single out one set of weights, we retain all sets
with a good enough fit as an ensemble rather than a single best fit. The strength
of the feedback loops, in contrast, shows mainly in how adoption develops
beyond the observed years, and is explored in sensitivity and scenario
analyses. Infrastructure and policy measures are choices rather than parameters to
estimate, and are explored in explicit scenarios. The design combines
established types of analysis for agent-based models
\citep{leeComplexitiesAgentBasedModeling2015} in five steps
(Figure~\ref{fig:method_flowchart}):

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt \parskip0pt
  \item \textbf{Calibrate plausible decision weights.} Candidate weight sets,
        sampled within the ranges from the literature, are scored against
        the observed adoption in 2022--2024, and the plausible ones are
        retained as an ensemble (Section~\ref{sec:calibrating}).
  \item \textbf{Rank the uncertain parameters.} Morris elementary effects
        establish which parameters most influence the 2050 heating mix.
  \item \textbf{Quantify the key parameters.} The highest-ranked parameters
        are varied jointly in a Latin-hypercube sample.
  \item \textbf{Run pathways and experiments.} The ensemble is carried
        through the scenarios at national scale, focusing on feedback loops,
        policy and infrastructure.
  \item \textbf{Analyze segments.} Results are broken down by adopter
        category, ownership and dwelling, to show which groups drive each
        dynamic.
\end{enumerate}

For the segment analysis, every owner-occupier household is assigned an
adopter category following Rogers' diffusion framework
\citep{rogersDiffusionInnovations2003a}, by percentile of an adoption index
that combines its sustainability attitude, the mean attitude of its peers and
the technical readiness of its dwelling, using
the standard shares (innovators 2.5\,\%, early adopters 13.5\,\%, early and
late majority 34\,\% each, laggards 16\,\%). Dwelling segments cross
archetype, floor area, construction era and insulation label, and are
additionally crossed with ownership and heat demand.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/fig_method_flowchart.png}
  \caption{The five-step analysis design. Calibration and parameter screening
    are carried out on the calibration region (Noord-Brabant). The scenario
    pathways and mechanism experiments are run for the full Netherlands.}
  \label{fig:method_flowchart}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Verification and validation}
\label{sec:verification}
The model is verified through unit tests on the decision functions, and a
check that the initialized 2022 heating mix matches the observed mix. 
It is documented following the ODD
protocol \citep{grimmODDProtocolReview2010} in \ref{app:model}. Consistent
with its purpose, the model is a tool to understand mechanisms and map
plausible pathways rather than to predict them. Therefore, matching the observed
2022--2024 change filters out implausible parameterizations but does not
validate the 2050 pathways.

\section{Calibrating to plausible decision weights}
\label{sec:calibrating}
We calibrate only the weights of the decision function and the share of the 
random term. They are central to
adoption behavior and thereby to the feedback loops. Also, they determine the
influence of the other uncertain assumptions. For example, when costs weigh little in the
decision, uncertainty in costs matters little as well. Calibrating all
parameters jointly would mainly reveal trade-offs between connected
assumptions rather than create a coherent bounded set of weights. The other 
parameters are explored in the sensitivity analysis instead.

The only consistent data set on the adoption of heating methods in the
Netherlands spans just three years (2022--2024). This short window cannot
uniquely identify the decision weights, as many combinations reproduce recent
adoption equally well. Rather than forcing a best fit and risking
overfitting, we use history matching within the Generalized Likelihood
Uncertainty Estimation (GLUE) framework \citep{beven_future_2006}. 
Each weight is sampled as a share within its stage of the decision
function, over its whole feasible range, so
that no prior judgement on the weights restricts the search. A
Latin-hypercube sample of 1000 weight sets is drawn across this space and
judged against the observations as described below.

Because each stage of the decision function is normalized by the sum of its
weights, only their ratios matter, and the weight space is fully covered by
four free parameters (attitude and subjective norm within intention,
affordability within PBC, intention within behavior). The scale $\lambda$ 
of the random term in the choice
(Equation~\ref{eq:argmax}) is calibrated together with the weights as a
fifth parameter. As the weights are normalized, $\lambda$ sets how often a
household chooses an option other than the one with the highest utility.
Because the utility differences between options are small and the current
system has the highest utility for most households, this largely determines
how many households switch in a given year.
It is sampled log-uniformly between 0.005 and 0.05. 
The retained sets form an ensemble that is carried into the
scenario analysis, so that pathways are reported as a band across
behaviorally plausible preferences rather than as a single fitted line.

Calibration is carried out on the province of Noord-Brabant over 2022--2024,
the most recent window for which the Dutch statistics office reports a
methodologically consistent per-neighborhood heating-mix series. The model is
initialized from the observed 2022 mix, and each weight set is scored on the
simulated 2023 and 2024 shares of natural gas boilers, hybrid and electric
heat pumps and district heating. Following standard practice in history
matching \citep{vernonGalaxyFormationBayesian2010}, as applied to agent-based
models by \citet{mccullochCalibratingAgentBasedModels2022}, a set is retained
unless one of these shares lies implausibly far from the observation, which is more
than three times the combined uncertainty of the model's stochastic
variation, the observation and the model's simplification, which amounts to
about 1.7 percentage points (\ref{app:calibration}).

\subsection{The calibrated weights set}
\label{sec:cal_weight_set}

Of the 1000 sampled weight sets, 124 are retained
(Figure~\ref{fig:ensemble_fit}), with a mean absolute deviation of 0.22--0.86
percentage points, against a median of 1.27 across all sampled sets. Above
the cutoff, the deviation levels off at about 2.9 percentage points for
around a third of the sets; in nearly all of these, the gas share stays too
high because owner-occupiers switch too slowly.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{figures/fig_ensemble_fit.png}
  \caption{Worst scored deviation from the observation of all 1000 sampled
  weight sets, ranked, with the retained ensemble in color.}
  \label{fig:ensemble_fit}
\end{figure}

Taken one at a time, the decision weights are hardly narrowed by the
observations, they constrain a combination of the weights instead
(Figure~\ref{fig:ensemble_structure}). The attitude and affordability
shares are strongly opposed, and require a sum which for the formula
$w_{\mathrm{cost}} + 2.0\,w_{\mathrm{att}}$, lies between 0.62 and 0.91
(Figure~\ref{fig:ensemble_structure}. 
This follows from the decision
function. In this early phase, effort and the subjective norm favor the
incumbent gas boiler, while attitude and, to a lesser extent, cost favor
heat pumps. As the effective weights sum to one, weight given to cost or
attitude is taken from effort and peers, so their combined weight determines
how many households switch. Too low a combination keeps the gas share too
high; too high a combination leads to too much adoption. Within these
bounds, the individual weights can take nearly their full range 
(Table~\ref{tab:ensemble}).

The observation window thus fixes how strongly owner-occupiers are pushed
towards a new heating method in total, not where that push comes from. Cost
and attitude substitute for each other, one unit of attitude weight does the
same as about two units of cost weight. The subjective norm hardly
contributes in this window, as the peer signal is weak while heat pumps are
still rare, which is why it is only bounded from above. The ensemble
therefore spans behaviorally distinct households, from cost-driven to
attitude-driven, that are expected to respond differently to price and
subsidy changes.

The observations do not pin the noise scale down either. In the retained
sets, between about 1 and 16\,\% (median 5.5\,\%) of the owner-occupier
decisions in 2023--2024 fall on an option other than the one with the highest
modeled utility. Simulating all 124 retained sets for Noord-Brabant until
2050 shows that the noise scale mainly affects hybrid heat pumps. Where more random
choice leads to more hybrids, which are often the close second option behind
a gas boiler or an electric heat pump.

\subsection{Selecting a subsample}
\label{sec:subsample}
Running all 124 retained sets through every scenario at national scale is not
feasible, so the scenario analysis uses a subsample of 25 sets, chosen so
that the median and the 5--95\,\% range of the baseline pathway in
Noord-Brabant match those of all retained sets (\ref{app:calibration}). The
resulting bands describe the spread across plausible weight sets, not
probability intervals. The sensitivity analysis draws its sets from the same
subsample. Because the influence of a parameter can depend on the weights,
the Morris screening is repeated at four sets that span the ensemble: the
best-fitting set, the most cost-driven and the most attitude-driven set, and
the set with the strongest subjective norm. The Latin-hypercube sample uses
the best-fitting set.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
%% ==========================================================================
The Netherlands is simulated over 2024--2050 for each of the 25 weight sets
across twenty scenarios. The sensitivity analysis is done for the province of
Noord-Brabant. Pathways are reported as ensemble bands and scenario effects
as the median across the ensemble. After the baseline, the results follow the
dynamics of Table~\ref{tab:dynamics}: the sensitivity analysis and the
feedback loops (Sections~\ref{sec:sensitivity} and
\ref{sec:res-feedback-loops}), the decisions of different groups of actors
(Section~\ref{sec:adoption_per_cat}), policy and infrastructure
(Section~\ref{sec:res-rules}), and an alternative decision structure
(Section~\ref{sec:pbc_split}).

The central result is that the decline of natural gas is robust. It occurs
in every scenario and for every weight set. How far it declines, and which
technologies replace it, depend on behavior, policy and infrastructure, and
no scenario phases out natural gas fully by 2050. In the
baseline (Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_with_bands}), natural gas falls from
86\,\% of dwellings in 2024 to a median of 15\,\% in 2050 (5--95\,\% range
7--27\,\%). Electric heat pumps become dominant (median 57\,\%, 47--70\,\%),
while hybrid heat pumps (16\,\%, 7--26\,\%) and district heating (11\,\%,
7--19\,\%) take smaller shares. The spread comes mainly from the trade-off
between the cost and attitude weights and from the noise scale
(Section~\ref{sec:cal_weight_set}).

\subsection{Sensitivity analysis}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

\subsubsection{Identifying relevant parameters using Morris elementary
effects}
\label{sec:morris}
We first rank the uncertain parameters with the Morris elementary-effects
method \citep{morris_factorial_1991,campolongoEffectiveScreeningDesign2007},
repeated at the four screening weight sets (Section~\ref{sec:subsample}). The
design, the parameters and their ranges are given in \ref{app:sensitivity}.
Figure~\ref{fig:morris} ranks the parameters by their effect on the 2050 mix,
colored by the part of the decision function they enter. Because the effect
is measured across the range of each parameter, the ranking depends on
the ranges. Those of the cost parameters are taken from the literature,
whereas those of the social and attitude parameters are modelling choices,
for lack of data.

Social learning has by far the largest effect, mainly on the gas share.
Stronger perceived norms favor the technology that currently dominates,
and thus reinforce the natural gas based regime. The
attitude distribution and the perceived sustainability of the hybrid heat
pump follow. This perceived sustainability decides between the two heat-pump
types. If hybrids are
seen as a credible intermediate, they are widely adopted. If they are not
perceived as a relatively sustainable alternative, the
electric heat pump is preferred. The learning rate, the heat-pump capital
cost, the opportunity trigger, the network size and the energy prices form a
second group, while the salience parameters matter much less. District
heating is insensitive to all parameters tested, as without strong policy
measures there is little adoption in any case.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=1  \linewidth]{figures/fig_structural_morris.png}
  \caption{Sensitivity of the 2050 heating mix in Noord-Brabant to the
  screened parameters, ranked by their total effect. Values are the mean
  absolute elementary effect $\mu^*$ of the Morris method, in percentage
  points. Which is the change in a technology's 2050 share when a parameter is moved
  across its range with all others held fixed, averaged in absolute
  value. The color strip shows the part of the decision function each
  parameter enters, structural parameters affect when a decision is made
  rather than how it is made.}
  \label{fig:morris}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Joint variations of key parameters}
The seven highest-ranked parameters are then varied jointly in a
Latin-hypercube sample \citep{mckayComparisonThreeMethods1979} of 80
combinations, eight iterations each, at the best-fitting weight set. Their
direction, magnitude and interactions are assessed by regression
\citep{saltelli_global_2008}. Together they spread the 2050 share of hybrid
heat pumps over 8--48\,\% and that of electric heat pumps over 35--74\,\%
(5--95\,\% range, Figure~\ref{fig:lhs_uncertainty}). The split between the
two heat pumps is set mainly by the perceived sustainability of the hybrid,
which across its range moves about 35 percentage points to hybrid heat
pumps and by the attitude
distribution. Greener attitudes add about 20 points to the electric heat
pump. The gas share, 2--22\,\%, depends on three parameters of similar
size, each moving it by 11--13 points across its range. Stronger social
learning and a later opportunity trigger keep more gas boilers in place,
while faster learning reduces them. Greener attitudes and cheaper heat
pumps reduce it by a further 6--8 points. District heating stays between 8
and 12\,\%, and the network size has little effect at this weight set. 
The parameters act largely independently, as a linear model explains
74--90\,\% of the variation in each share.
Interactions matter mainly for gas, raising this to 95\,\%, chiefly between
the learning rate and the heat-pump capital cost and between social
learning and the opportunity trigger.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/fig_lhs_uncertainty.png}
  \caption{Spread of the 2050 heating mix in Noord-Brabant across 80
  combinations of the seven highest-ranked parameters (Latin-hypercube
  sample, eight iterations each), at the best-fitting weight set. The graph
  shows the full range, 5--95\,\% and 25--75\,\% ranges, and median.}
  \label{fig:lhs_uncertainty}
\end{figure}

Taken together, the screening and the joint variation separate robust
from uncertain outcomes. In every combination, natural gas declines
without disappearing and district heating stays small without policy.
How far gas declines depends mainly on social learning, economic
learning and the timing of replacement. Across the ensemble, social
learning is the strongest of these, but its effect depends on the
weight households give to the subjective norm. It dominates where that
weight is high and vanishes where decisions are driven by cost, whereas
the effect of economic learning is similar across weight sets. The
split between hybrid and electric heat pumps depends mainly on how
sustainable the hybrid is perceived to be and on households' attitudes.
The parameters that matter most are thus the social and attitudinal
ones whose ranges rest on modelling choices rather than data, which
makes them a priority for empirical research. Their effect on the 2050
mix is of the same order as that of the uncertainty in the decision
weights.

\subsection{Feedback loops}
\label{sec:res-feedback-loops}
The sensitivity analysis shows that the parameters driving the economic and
social learning loops are key to the results. To understand when and by whom
these dynamics are triggered, we vary the strength of each loop in the
scenario analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:loop_scenarios}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]
    {figures/fig_scenario_mix_loops_grid.png}
  \caption{Heating mix 2024--2050 in the feedback-loop scenarios (median
  across the 25 weight sets). Scenario's include weak and strong social and
  economic learning and the two loops combined in opposite
  directions. Stronger social learning favors the status quo, and stronger
  economic learning reduces natural gas.}
  \label{fig:loop_scenarios}
\end{figure}

Social learning is the strongest lever, in line with the sensitivity
analysis. It favors the status quo, with strong social learning, 26\,\% of
dwellings still heat with natural gas in 2050, against 10\,\% with weak
social learning and 15\,\% in the baseline. The 2050 share of electric heat
pumps differs by only half a percentage point, so the difference in gas is
taken up mainly by hybrid heat pumps and partly by
district heating. Most of the effect, however, lies in the
pathway rather than in the 2050 shares (Figure~\ref{fig:loop_divergence}).
With strong social learning, electric heat pumps fall up to 8 percentage
points behind the baseline around 2036, but this gap has nearly closed by
2050, whereas the gas share keeps diverging until the early 2040s and remains
above the baseline in 2050. Peer influence first favors
the dominant technology, but as electric heat pumps spread, the norm shifts
towards them.

The economic learning loop has no threshold of its own, as every installed
unit slightly reduces the costs, and because households differ widely in
energy demand and insulation costs, the resulting adoption curves are smooth.
Strong economic learning reduces the 2050 share of natural gas to 8\,\%,
against 19\,\% with weak learning. It interacts with other cost drivers, with
low gas and high electricity prices, 21\,\% of dwellings still use natural
gas in 2050, against 7\,\% with high gas and low electricity prices. In the
scenario most favorable to natural gas, with strong social learning, weak
economic learning and cheap gas, 41\,\% of dwellings still heat with gas in
2050, against 2\,\% in the scenario most favorable to heat pumps.

The two loops do not reinforce each other, their effects roughly add up. With
weak economic and strong social learning, 31\,\% of dwellings still use
natural gas in 2050. In the opposite combination, 5\,\%. Even in the most
favorable scenario, natural gas does not disappear.
None of the feedback loops has a
strong effect on district heating, whose 2050 share stays between 8 and
12\,\%, indicating that heat networks will not expand without policy action.

  feedback-loop scenarios, in percentage points (median across the 25 weight
  sets).}

\end{figure}

\subsection{Adoption per adopter and dwelling category}
\label{sec:adoption_per_cat}
To see which groups drive these dynamics, we analyze the 2050 heating mix per
segment in the baseline scenario (Figure~\ref{fig:segments_2050}). The
figures in this section show the best-fitting weight set, how the outcome
splits between ownership types differs between weight sets.

Ownership determines which decision rule applies
(Figure~\ref{fig:segments_2050}a). Owner-occupiers, who weigh attitudes and
norms as well as costs, end with the largest share of hybrid heat pumps
(34\,\%) and almost no natural gas (3\,\%). Housing associations and private
landlords, who decide on cost alone, electrify at least as much (60 and
58\,\% electric heat pumps, against 51\,\% for owner-occupiers), but where
the electric heat pump is not the cheapest option they keep their gas
boilers, 12 and 10\,\% of their dwellings still use natural gas in 2050. This
shows how these actors would act without the sustainability requirements,
rules and incentives they face in practice. District heating reaches
12--13\,\% of owner-occupied and social-housing dwellings, but only 8\,\% of
privately rented dwellings and none of the dwellings in owner associations.

Within the owner-occupied stock, the adopter category separates the outcome
far more than any dwelling characteristic (Figure~\ref{fig:segments_2050}b).
Innovators, early adopters and the early majority choose the electric heat
pump almost exclusively (83--92\,\%). The late majority turns mainly to
hybrid heat pumps (60\,\%), and the laggards almost
entirely so (67\,\% hybrid), while 17\,\% of the laggards
still heat with natural gas in 2050. The lagging half of the owner-occupiers
thus act in the transition, but mainly by means of hybrid heat pumps.

Heat demand and dwelling type mainly shift the choice between hybrid heat
pumps and district heating (Figure~\ref{fig:segments_2050}c--d).
Owner-occupied dwellings with a low heat demand, often apartments and
high-rise, connect more often to district heating, whereas dwellings with a
high demand take more hybrids, while the share of
electric heat pumps hardly changes. The insulation label
separates the outcome more strongly (Figure~\ref{fig:segments_2050}e). 
Two-thirds of dwellings with label A or B in 2024 have an electric heat pump
in 2050, against one-third of those with label E to G.

  weight set. (a) ownership, (b) adopter category of owner-occupiers, (c--d)

  \label{fig:segments_2050}
\end{figure}

The adoption process is not finished in 2050 (Figure~\ref{fig:segments}).
Electric heat pumps spread rapidly among the innovators and early adopters
and level off around 2040 at about 90\,\%. The early majority follows a few
years later. In the late majority, electric heat pumps reach about a quarter
of the dwellings around 2045 and then stagnate, while hybrid adoption in the
late majority and the laggards is still increasing in 2050, and
district heating grows from about 4 to 15\,\%.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/mech_segment_scurves.png}
  \caption{Adoption by adopter category, per technology, as a share of each
  segment's own stock, in the baseline scenario for the best-fitting weight
  set.}
  \label{fig:segments}
\end{figure}

Comparing the cost-optimal option, with the option of highest utility, and the
actually adopted option, shows a familiar transition dynamic
(Figure~\ref{fig:decision_mix}). Electric and hybrid heat pumps are already
the cost-optimal option for three quarters of installations in 2030, yet
natural gas has the highest utility in more than half of them. Through
attitude, social norm and effort, owner-occupiers stay with the incumbent
system even when an alternative is cheaper. The gap closes as attitudes and
norms towards heat pumps grow. Because of the random term, a dwelling whose
highest-utility option leads only narrowly may choose the runner-up. Natural
gas is involved in most of these close decisions, especially early-on in the
transition.

  (before the random term) and adopted heating system in the baseline
  scenario, as a share of the dwellings that install a heating system in that

  \label{fig:decision_mix}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Policy and infrastructure}
\label{sec:res-rules}
Next, we test whether policy measures and infrastructure can trigger or stop
these dynamics. We start this section with exploring the impact of a ban on 
electric heat pumps in neighborhoods with grid
congestion, which arises from the combined load of heat pumps and electric
vehicles. This does not slow the transition away from gas and does not change the
share of district heating (Figure~\ref{fig:scenario_congestion}). Instead, it
substitutes the share of electric heat pumps for hybrids. 
Households that cannot install an electric heat pump turn to
its closest alternative, not only in cost but also in attitude and norm. In 
the model, congestion spreads in the early 2030s, reaching about half of all 
neighborhoods by 2035. This is later than in reality,
where it is an immediate issue, but the dynamic makes the hybrid heat pump a
potential transition technology where the grid requires it.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_scenario_mix_congestion.png}
  \caption{Heating mix 2024--2050 with and without a ban on electric heat
  pumps in congested neighborhoods (median across the 25 weight sets).}
  \label{fig:scenario_congestion}
\end{figure}

District heating hardly expands on cost alone. We therefore test two further
scenarios. First, heat networks built in the areas selected by municipalities,
regardless of household demand. Second, these same networks combined with a
connection obligation building on new regulations towards district heating
(Figure~\ref{fig:scenario_district_heating_rollout}).
When connecting remains voluntary, few households connect and district
heating ends at about the baseline share. Only with an obligation does
district heating expand, to about 40\,\% of dwellings by 2050, mainly at
the expense of the electric heat pump. Putting the infrastructure in place is
therefore not enough, an obligation, stronger incentives to connect, or much
cheaper district heating tariffs are required.

\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_scenario_mix_rollout.png}
  \caption{Heating mix 2024--2050 in the baseline, with the heat networks
  planned by municipalities, and with the same networks combined with a
  connection obligation (median across the 25 weight sets).}
  \label{fig:scenario_district_heating_rollout}
\end{figure}

Finally, we examine who pays for the energy networks. Under current Dutch
tariffs, the fixed costs of a network are paid by whoever is connected to it.
Households on district heating pay for their heat network and also for their
electricity and sometimes gas connection, whereas households with an electric
heat pump pay only for the electricity grid. This allocation is part of an
ongoing political debate \citep{rondeSocialiserenVanKosten2025,
MaakHetWarmtenet}, and electricity tariffs will become partly
consumption-based from 2029 \citep{marktACMPubliceertVoorstel2026,
EindrapporttijdsEnvolumeafhankelijkenettarievenenwarmtepompenVWP1}. We
explore the far end of this debate with a scenario in which all gas and
district-heating connection costs are removed, which is equivalent to
spreading them evenly across all households, while equipment investments and
energy prices are unchanged. 
The removed costs are the annual gas connection charge (transport and
fixed charge), the annual district-heating charges (fixed charge, metering
and delivery-set rental) and the one-off district-heating connection and
disconnection fees The result is a substantially different heating
system (Figure~\ref{fig:scenario_connection_costs}). By 2050 the share of
electric heat pumps falls from 57 to 17\,\%, while that of hybrid heat pumps
rises from 16 to 33\,\% and that of district heating from 11 to 40\,\%.
Without the gas charge, the hybrid heat pump, with its lower investment,
becomes cheaper than the electric heat pump. Also, without its connection
charges, district heating becomes the cheapest option for many dwellings
within reach of a network. The shift is carried mainly by housing
associations and landlords, who decide on cost. Among owner-occupiers,
attitudes and peers keep about half of the decisions in which the hybrid has
become cheapest with the electric heat pump.

\end{figure}

\subsection{Split investment and running costs in decision-making}
\label{sec:pbc_split}
Lastly, we explore structural uncertainty in the decision function itself. An
often mentioned barrier is the high upfront investment in heat pumps
\citep{jagerHouseholdHeatPump2025,dejongAreDutchHomeowners2024, gaurHeatPumpsOur2021,
ebrahimigharehbaghiUnravellingDutchHomeowners2019},
especially when a renovation is required \citep{lambIncreasingHeatPump2024}. 
Even when their equivalent annual
costs are lower, households may be unable or unwilling to pay the investment.
We test this by splitting the cost term in the PBC (Equation~\ref{eq:pbc})
into the annual running costs and the upfront investment costs. Each with its
own weight, and recalibrating the weights on the same observations. The split
structure fits the observations as well as the original one, and the
cost--attitude trade-off reappears, now carried by the running-cost weight.
The decline of natural gas is nearly unchanged, but district heating rises
about 7\,\%, at the expense of electric heat pumps (Figure~\ref{fig:scenario_pbc_split}).
District heating, with its subsidized connection fee, has low upfront but
relatively high running costs, so it gains wherever households weigh the
investment separately. While electric heat pumps have large investment costs,
especially for those dwellings who require renovations.

\end{figure}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%% ==========================================================================
This paper shows how bottom-up modelling, grounded in individual decisions in
a heterogeneous population, can be used to explore the transition pathways of
an entire sector. It produces non-linear adoption that can be studied per
population group, ownership type and dwelling segment, and it allows a broad
exploration of the solution space. Such models should nevertheless be used
with caution. Actual decision-making is more elaborate and context-specific,
and people use different decision strategies
\citep{michelsenMotivationalFactorsInfluencing2013} with large heterogeneity
in preferences \citep{melesHeterogeneityPreferencesRenewable2022}, and a
decision as consequential as a heating system usually evolves over time
through social discourse and information seeking. The uncertainty of such
processes has led most energy-transition pathway studies to omit them. We
argue that it is better to include them and explore their influence across a
wide range of plausible parameterizations, which leads to the following
points.

\emph{Social reinforcement favors the incumbent.} Strengthening social
learning slows the transition rather than accelerating it. This runs against
the common framing of social influence as a lever for accelerating low-carbon
adoption, in which peer effects and social norms are mechanisms to be
amplified \citep{wiedermannNetworkbasedMicrofoundationGranovetters2020,
lentonOperationalisingPositiveTipping2022/ed}. The contradiction is caused by
the direction of the mechanism. Social influence
reflects what peers currently do, which at the start of the transition is the
incumbent system. Strong social learning therefore delays the spread of
electric heat pumps. Once they have gained ground, the norm turns in their
favor and the delay is largely made up by 2050. Although norms form within
local, small-world networks, the net effect is still driven by the majority.
This would only differ if social influence were asymmetric, with adopters of
a new technology weighing more heavily as peers than users of the incumbent,
which can be explored with the same model in future work.

\emph{The two loops work differently.} Economic learning acts in the same
direction from the start: every installed heat pump lowers the cost of the
next, and adoption follows smoothly, without a threshold. Social learning
follows the current majority and therefore changes direction during the
transition. Combined, the two loops add up rather than amplify each other,
while the parameters within a loop do interact.

\emph{Rules and policies shape the route.} Natural gas declines in every
scenario and weight set but disappears in none, and under current rules the
electric heat pump becomes the dominant alternative across all plausible
decision weights. Rules and infrastructure change the route where hybrids
are often a good second-best alternative between natural gas and all-electric, 
and district heating requires strong policy support.

\emph{Social and psychological parameters remain hard to pin down.} The
parameters that most widen the range of pathways describe social influence,
perceived sustainability, and attitudes, which are also the parameters with
the least empirical support. The same holds for the decision structure, how
households weigh upfront investment against running costs decides between
electric heat pumps and district heating, while these differences are very
context and individual dependent. Better data on how households perceive and discuss heating
options would narrow the uncertainty more than better cost data, in line
with calls for the empirical validation of behavioral insights in agent-based
models \citep{rietkerkBridgingBehavioralTheory2025}. However, inherent to the
complexity of decision-making, uncertainty will remain. Models can be used to
explore different pathways while acknowledging this uncertainty.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\emph{The calibration window is short and atypical.} Two years of observed
change bound the decision weights only partly, which is why we report an
ensemble rather than a fitted parameterization. The window coincides with the
gas-price crisis and strong subsidies, while the model holds energy prices at
their 2025 level over this period.

\emph{Matching the recent past does not validate the projections.} The
calibration filters out implausible parameterizations but does not establish
that the 2050 pathways are correct. These are interpreted for the mechanisms
they reveal, not as forecasts.

\emph{Structural uncertainty is explored only partly.} We test one
alternative form of the cost perception (Section~\ref{sec:pbc_split}), but
not alternatives to the theory of planned behavior as a whole or to the
description of social visibility, and the sensitivity results are conditional
on parameter ranges that are partly modelling choices.

\emph{Some mechanisms are represented schematically.} Peer networks include
owner-occupiers only, district heating has no economic learning and a
regulated tariff, and landlords and housing
associations are simplified cost-minimizing agents. The segment analysis 
reports a single weight set, and the sensitivity analysis is performed for 
one province. Additional detail in all these cases would be possible in future
studies, this way, enabling scholars to dive deeper into the adoption patterns
of the empirical dwelling stock finding more transition dynamics that could
be used to guide policy decisions.


\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We set out to answer two questions: which features of the Dutch residential
heat transition are robust across plausible behavior and which depend on
behavior, policy and infrastructure, and what an empirically grounded
bottom-up model can contribute to transition planning when data are limited
and future decision-making is inherently uncertain.

On the first question, the decline of natural gas is robust, but its
replacement is not. Gas declines in every scenario and weight set but
disappears in none, and under current rules the electric heat pump becomes
the main alternative across all plausible decision weights. How far gas
declines and what replaces it depend on behavior, policy and infrastructure.
Strong social learning keeps gas in place for longer, a congestion ban
redirects adoption to hybrid heat pumps, heat networks are used at scale only
under a connection obligation, and the allocation of network costs can shift
the mix from electric heat pumps to hybrids and district heating.

On the second question, such a model is most useful not for forecasting a
single pathway, but for separating robust from contingent developments,
locating the barriers to desired outcomes and showing what it takes to align
household decisions with policy goals. Four features of the approach make
this possible. First, retaining an ensemble of plausible decision weights
instead of a single calibrated parameterization makes the limits of the data
explicit. Two years of observed adoption admit 124 of 1000 sampled weight
sets, which agree that gas declines but differ in how far and in the split
between electric and hybrid heat pumps. Carrying them through all experiments
separates no-regret developments from outcomes that depend on assumptions.
Second, representing decisions and feedback loops explicitly reveals
mechanisms that aggregate models miss. Social learning follows the current
majority and therefore delays electrification early in the transition,
whereas economic learning favors heat pumps from the start.
Third, the bottom-up structure shows whom
policy needs to reach. The leading half of owner-occupiers electrifies almost
completely, the late majority and laggards turn mainly to hybrid heat pumps,
and landlords and housing associations keep gas where the electric heat pump
is not the cheapest option. Fourth, the sensitivity analysis shows where
better data would help most. The parameters that most widen the range of
pathways, such as social influence, perceived sustainability and attitudes,
are the least supported empirically.

For policy, timing measures matters as much as instrument choice. Cost-side
measures move households away from gas at every stage of the transition,
social instruments pay off once sustainable heating has a foothold, and new
infrastructure needs obligations or incentives to be used. Further work
should collect data on these social and psychological parameters, test
alternative decision-model forms, including heterogeneous and evolving
frameworks, and study spill-overs between ownership groups and the decisions
of landlords and housing associations.

\section{Model documentation}
\label{app:model}

This appendix documents the model following the ODD (Overview, Design
concepts, Details) protocol \citep{grimmODDProtocolReview2010}. Cost,
learning and scenario details are given in
Appendices~\ref{app:cost}--\ref{app:grid}. Here we summarize the structure
and refer back to the main text for the decision equations.

\subsection{ODD protocol}
\label{app:odd}



\subsubsection{Overview}

\paragraph{Purpose and patterns}
The purpose of the model is to explore which features of the 2024--2050
heating-technology pathways of the Dutch residential heat transition are
robust across plausible household behavior, and which depend on behavior,
policy and infrastructure. To this end, it simulates the heating decisions
of owner-occupiers, landlords and housing associations for every dwelling,
together with economic and social learning, grid congestion and the
expansion of heat networks. The model is not meant to predict a single
pathway. Because the available adoption data cannot pin down the decision
weights, it is run with an ensemble of plausible weight sets. Its pathways
are used to identify developments that hold across this ensemble, the
barriers to desired outcomes, and the policies and infrastructure that
change the route of the transition.

The model is evaluated against three patterns. First, low-carbon heating
technologies should diffuse non-linearly, driven by cost decline and social
influence. Second, adoption timing and technology choice should differ
across household types, ownership and dwelling characteristics. Third, the
simulated change in the provincial heating mix over 2022--2024 should match
the observed change in direction and magnitude. This pattern is used to
select plausible weight sets during calibration
(Section~\ref{sec:calibrating}).

\paragraph{Entities, state variables and scales}
The model contains six entity types: dwellings, owner-occupiers, social
housing associations, landlords, a district heating company and an
electricity grid operator. On top of this there are neighborhoods as the spatial unit at which
collective (grid and district-heating) decisions are made, and streets or housing blocks as
the unit on which housing association and block heating decisions are made.
Table~\ref{tab:odd-state} lists the principal state variables per entity.
Global state comprises, per heating technology, the learning-updated
investment cost, cumulative installed units, energy price and salience
factor. The model advances in annual time steps over 2024--2050 (and over
2022--2024 for calibration). The spatial extent is a single province (the
calibration and analysis scope, e.g.\ Noord-Brabant) or the full Netherlands
($\sim$8~million dwellings). Dwellings are located to neighborhoods but
not to coordinates.

\paragraph{Process overview and scheduling}
Each simulated year executes the following schedule
(Figure~\ref{fig:flowchart}):
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Ageing and exogenous change.} All heating systems age by one
    year. A fraction of dwellings receive exogenous insulation and
    heat-distribution upgrades (independent of the heating decision).
  \item \textbf{District-heating expansion.} The district heating company
    evaluates each neighborhood and starts (or continues) grid construction
    according to its strategy.
  \item \textbf{Adoption decisions.} Actors decide in a fixed order.
    Social-housing and owners'-association blocks decide first, followed by
    landlords. Both are triggered when the heating system reaches the end of
    its life. Under the policy-based strategy, a social-housing block in a
    neighborhood planned for district heating also connects in the year the
    heat network arrives. Owner-occupiers decide last, triggered by the end
    of life or, past 75\,\% of the system's life, by an opportunity.
    Dwellings on district heating stay connected. Owner-occupiers are
    processed in two passes. The first computes the equivalent annual cost
    of every option for every triggered owner-occupier, so that the cost
    scale used to normalize affordability covers all decisions of that year.
    The second evaluates the theory-of-planned-behavior utility and makes a
    random-utility choice.
  \item \textbf{Grid reaction.} The grid operator recomputes peak electricity
    demand per neighborhood and reinforces congested neighborhoods up to its
    annual rate.
  \item \textbf{Global updates.} Technology salience and the economic
    learning curves are updated from the year's installations, feeding the
    next year's decisions.
\end{enumerate}
State is updated asynchronously within a year (an agent's decision is visible
to agents acting later that year), while learning and salience are updated
synchronously at year-end.

\subsubsection{Design concepts}

\paragraph{Basic principles}
Household adoption follows the theory of planned behavior
\citep{ajzenTheoryPlannedBehavior1991}, based on the application by \citet{okurAdoptionRenewableHeating2024} embedded in a discrete
random-utility choice. System-level change is driven by economic learning
(Wright's law), social learning (a salience-mediated subjective norm), and
threshold-based collective infrastructure decisions. These generate the
transition dynamics described in Section~\ref{sec:dynamics}.

Because the underpinning data are limited, uncertainty is large, and the
analysis design treats each type of uncertainty separately
(Table~\ref{tab:uncertainty}). Stochastic variability arises at the level of
individual households and largely averages out over the many dwellings in a
run, so that few replications are required. The calibration and the national
scenarios use two, the Morris screening one and the Latin-hypercube sample
eight. Parameters that act in the 2022--2024 observation window mostly act on
the same terms as the decision weights. For example, the attitude
distribution and the perceived sustainability of each heating method act on
the attitude term. When the weights are free they compensate, so calibrating
these parameters jointly would widen the trade-offs between them rather than
constrain them. They are therefore held at their defaults in the calibration
and varied afterwards in the sensitivity analysis. Only the scale of the
random term is calibrated with the weights, because it scales the whole
utility rather than one term of it.

\paragraph{Emergence}
Aggregate heating-mix pathways, including their accelerations, delays and
lock-ins, emerge from individual decisions and their feedback mechanisms. 
Prices, costs and social norms that individual agents respond to are
themselves outcomes of the population's decisions.

\paragraph{Adaptation}
Triggered owner-occupiers adapt by selecting the option with the highest
perceived utility among those technically available to them. Landlords and
social housing associations adapt by selecting the lowest
equivalent-annual-cost option. Infrastructure agents adapt their expansion to
demand and policy.

\paragraph{Objectives}
Owner-occupiers maximize a composite utility (affordability, ease,
attitude, and social conformity). Landlords and associations minimize the annual
cost. The district heating company implicitly pursues a positive business case, or
follows policy. The grid operator maintains capacity.

\paragraph{Learning}
Two reinforcing loops operate. Economic learning lowers a technology's
investment cost as its cumulative installed base grows (Wright's law). Social
learning raises a technology's salience, and thereby the subjective norm
favoring it, as its adoption share grows. Both loops can turn a marginal
advantage into self-sustaining growth.

\paragraph{Prediction}
Agents are myopic, they do not forecast future prices, costs or adoption.
Each decision uses the current year's costs and prices, held constant over
the equipment's economic lifetime when computing the equivalent annual cost.

\paragraph{Sensing}
Owner-occupiers sense the current heating methods of the peers in their
social network and the availability of options (whether a district-heating
grid exists in their neighborhood, and whether grid congestion blocks
electric heat pumps). All agents sense the current technology costs and their
own dwelling's characteristics.

\paragraph{Interaction}
Direct interaction is limited to owner-occupiers observing network peers.
Most interaction is indirect through the shared learning curve, through 
social norms, through the district-heating grid (a collective asset), and
through grid congestion (a shared constraint). Social housing associations
interact strongly with the collective actors by shifting whole neighborhoods
at once.

\paragraph{Stochasticity}
Stochasticity enters at initialization (household attitudes, peer-network
formation, the assignment of ownership and current heating method from
neighborhood statistics, and each system's end-of-life age, drawn from a
normal distribution around its nominal lifetime) and at decision time (a
Gumbel random-utility term that lets choices deviate from the utility
optimum). Results are therefore reported as means and spreads over
replications and weight sets (Table~\ref{tab:uncertainty}).

\paragraph{Collectives}
Two collectives are represented explicitly. A social-housing block is the set
of an association's dwellings within one postcode (roughly a street or
complex) that decide and switch together. Home-owners' association blocks do the
same for dwellings on collective gas heating. Neighborhoods are collectives
for infrastructure, the district-heating grid and grid congestion are
neighborhood-level states that all resident dwellings share.

\paragraph{Observation}
For each scenario and year, the model records the
installed stock and the annual installed/removed numbers of each heating method
by ownership category, together with the mean of each decision term
(attitude, subjective norm, perceived behavioral control, utility) and the
mean equivalent annual cost. Derived outputs include technology-share
pathways, their breakdown by segment, and the mix of cost-optimal,
highest-utility and chosen options.

\subsubsection{Details}

\paragraph{Initialization}
The dwelling stock is constructed from Dutch building administration and
neighborhood statistics (Section~\ref{method}). Each dwelling is assigned a
construction year, type and floor area, an ownership category
(owner-occupier, social housing or landlord), an initial heating method,
insulation label, and heat-distribution system drawn from the observed
neighborhood heating mix of the initialization year, and an end-of-life age
for its current system drawn around the system's lifetime.

Ownership combines two resolutions. The neighborhood shares of
owner-occupied, housing-corporation and other rental dwellings
\citep{cbsKerncijfersWijkenBuurten2023} fix how many dwellings of each
category a neighborhood contains, so that provincial totals reproduce the
national housing-stock statistics \citep{cbsVoorraadWoningenEigendom2025} to
within half a percentage point. Postcode-level (PC6, about 20 dwellings)
figures \citep{cbsKerncijfersPostcode2023} decide where they are located.
Because housing corporations own whole streets and complexes rather than
scattered dwellings, social housing is placed in clusters. Each postcode
first receives its reported number of corporation dwellings, and the
remainder of the neighborhood's social stock fills postcodes without a
reported count one complete postcode at a time, in a random order weighted by
their rental share. Postcodes without rental dwellings receive none. Private
rental is then drawn per dwelling from the remaining stock, weighted by the
postcode's private-rental share, and the rest is owner-occupied. The social
dwellings of each postcode form one housing block that decides collectively.
Each block takes a single initial heating method from the neighborhood mix,
with multi-family blocks served first because district and collective heating
are concentrated in apartment buildings. Owner-occupied and privately rented
dwellings on collective gas heating form owners'-association blocks that
likewise decide collectively. Owner-occupier sustainability attitudes are
drawn from a distribution parameterized from
\citet{okurAdoptionRenewableHeating2024} and
\citet{broersDecidedDividedEmpirical2019} and correlated with peer-network
and insulation state through Cholesky decomposition
\citep{edlinCorrelatedParametersCholesky2015}. Owner-occupiers are then
connected in small-world networks that preferentially link households of
similar attitude within the same municipality \citep{loomansQuantifyingPotentialEnergy2026}.
Table~\ref{tab:network} shows for 20 random owner-occupiers that the
resulting networks link mainly households of the same adopter category.
Technology salience is seeded
from the initial stock shares, and cumulative installed bases are set to the
initial stock so the learning curve starts from the present.

\paragraph{Input data}
Building characteristics are from the Dutch cadastre
\citep{kadasterDatasetBasisregistratieAdressen2022}; heat demand, insulation
costs and energy-label transitions from VestaMAIS \citep{pblHetVestaMAIS2017,
vandermolenFunctioneelOntwerpVesta2021}; ownership from the CBS neighborhood
and postcode key figures \citep{cbsKerncijfersWijkenBuurten2023,
cbsKerncijfersPostcode2023}; observed per neighborhood heating mixes and
their recent change from CBS; observed consumer energy prices from CBS
\citep{StatLineGemiddeldeEnergietarievena} and their trajectories from the national
Climate and Energy Outlook \citep{pbl_kev_2025}; district-heating tariffs
from the ACM \citep{marktTarievenWarmteKoude}; and technology investment
cost, lifetime, efficiency, subsidy and learning-rate assumptions as listed
in Appendix~\ref{app:cost}.

\paragraph{Submodels}
\emph{Triggering.} A decision is triggered when a system reaches the end of
its life (problem-based) or, for owner-occupiers only, once its age
exceeds 75\,\% of its life (opportunity-based). The trigger type governs
whether an agent evaluates options, not the option costs directly. A dwelling
on district heating has no end of life, it is reviewed every five years since
connecting and only changes if another option is chosen.
\emph{Owner-occupier adoption} evaluates, for each available option,
attitude, subjective norm and perceived behavioral
control, combines them into an intention and a utility
(Equations~\ref{eq:sn}--\ref{eq:utility}), and selects the highest-utility
available option with an added Gumbel term (Equation~\ref{eq:argmax}).
\emph{Landlords, housing associations and owners' associations} select the
option with the lowest equivalent annual cost (Equation~\ref{eq:eac},
Appendix~\ref{app:cost}), with a small Gumbel term. Blocks use the
household-averaged cost and switch as a whole. Social housing associations
may instead follow the neighborhood policy plan.
\emph{District-heating expansion} builds a grid in a neighborhood once the
heat demand of interested dwellings crosses the positive-business-case
threshold ($\sim$600\,GJ/ha) or, under the policy strategy, where the
heat-transition-vision plan specifies, after a construction lead time.
\emph{Grid reinforcement} recomputes neighborhood peak demand (including an
exogenous electric-vehicle load, see ~\ref{app:grid}) and reinforces congested neighborhoods up to
the annual reinforcement rate. \emph{Economic learning} updates each
technology's investment cost by Wright's law from its cumulative
installations (Equation~\ref{eq:learning}), scaled by the scenario learning
factor. \emph{Salience}, which enters the subjective norm
(Equation~\ref{eq:sn}), is updated each year from the population share
$x_{hm}$ of method $hm$ by an S-curve combining a novelty term (favoring
low-share methods) and a momentum term (favoring methods whose share is
rising):

\begin{equation}\label{eq:salience}
  \sigma_{hm} = m\,\nu + (1-m)\,x_{hm},\qquad
  \nu = \frac{1}{1+e^{\,k(x_{hm}-\theta)}},\qquad
  m = \frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta\,(x_{hm}-x_{hm,t-1})}}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\theta$ is the novelty threshold, $k$ its steepness and
$\beta$ the momentum steepness. Finally, the affordability term in
Equation~\ref{eq:pbc} normalizes the equivalent annual cost on a logarithmic
min--max scale spanning all methods and dwellings available in the year, so
that a household's decision reflects the \emph{proportional} cost differences
between its options rather than being dominated by the most expensive
dwellings. Scenario submodels (grid reinforcement rate, district-heating
strategy, connection obligations and the congestion heat-pump ban) are
detailed in ~\ref{app:scenario-input}.

\subsection{Cost calculations}
\label{app:cost}

\subsubsection{EAC calculations}

The costs are calculated using the Equivalent Annual Costs (EAC) for a fair
comparison between heating methods with different investment costs,
operational costs, and lifetimes \citep{hastingsFinancialMethods2021}.
Furthermore, the costs depend on the required insulation levels and in-house
heat distribution system. For all-electric heat pumps, low-temperature
in-house heat distribution and a high level of insulation is required for
efficient operation. If these are not yet installed, additional costs are
added to the investment costs. This does of course lead to reduced heat
demand, which decreases costs. These cost calculations are made for all
dwellings when the adoption functions are triggered, independent of their
owner.

The equivalent annual cost of option $j$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eac}
  EAC_j = \frac{1}{A_{T,r}}\left(I_j - S_j
  + \sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{C_j}{(1+r)^{t}}\right),
  \qquad A_{T,r} = \frac{1-(1+r)^{-T}}{r},
\end{equation}
with $I_j$ the investment, $S_j$ the subsidy, $C_j$ the annual running
cost, $T$ the lifetime and $r$ the discount rate. The discount rate is
2\,\% over 15 years for the individual systems and 3\,\% over 30 years for
a district-heating connection. The investment is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ic-total}
  I_j = IC_{sys,j} + IC_{dist} + IC_{ins}.
\end{equation}
Here $IC_{sys,j}$ is the cost of the system itself. It is a fixed price
for the gas boiler, follows Equation~\ref{eq:ic-hp} for the heat pumps and
is the connection fee for district heating. $IC_{dist}$ and $IC_{ins}$ are
the costs of a low-temperature distribution system and of insulation to
the required label (Equation~\ref{eq:ic-ins}), added only when the option
requires them and the dwelling lacks them. The annual running cost is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:c-annual}
  C_j = \sum_{i}\frac{f_{ij}\,Q}{\eta_{ij}}\,p_i + M_j + F_j,
\end{equation}
with $Q$ the heat demand of the dwelling, $f_{ij}$ the share of heat from
energy carrier $i$, $\eta_{ij}$ its conversion efficiency, $p_i$ its price,
$M_j$ the maintenance cost and $F_j$ the fixed connection charges.

For district heating the terms differ (Table~\ref{tab:dh-costs}): $IC_{heat}$
is the connection fee for a dwelling that newly connects and zero for one
that is already connected, and the fixed charge, metering fee and
delivery-set rental are annual costs. A dwelling that leaves district heating
adds the disconnection fee to the investment cost of its new system. The heat
price is per kWh delivered, so no conversion efficiency applies.

The individual heating systems are parameterized from the cost figures that
CE Delft compiled for the PBL model Vesta MAIS
\citep{cedelftUpdateKentallenInstallaties2023} (Table~\ref{tab:hs-costs}).
These are given excluding VAT at 2020 prices and are converted to constant
2024 euros including VAT with the consumer price index, as the energy prices
are. The investment itself is set at the current consumer prices of Milieu
Centraal, because the CE Delft figures, a 2020 cost price indexed with
general inflation whose upper bound is a standalone replacement, lie
25--50\,\% above them for heat pumps. A gas boiler costs 2\,100\,EUR
including installation \citep{milieucentraalCvKetelVervangen2026} whatever
the heat demand, because a combi boiler is chosen by its hot-water class
rather than its heating capacity. The heat pumps are priced by capacity,
because their cost rises with the capacity a dwelling needs:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ic-hp}
  IC_{hp} = IC_{0} + c_{kW}\,P, \qquad P =
  \min\!\left(P_{max},\,\max\!\left(P_{min},\,
  s\,\frac{Q}{h_{fl}}\right)\right)
\end{equation}
with $Q$ the dwelling's heat demand, $h_{fl}$ the full-load hours, $s$ the
share of the peak covered by the heat pump (1 for the electric heat pump, 0.5
for the hybrid, whose capacity is limited to 2--6\,kW) and $c_{kW}$ the cost
per kW used in Vesta MAIS 5.0 (410\,EUR/kW for an air-to-water heat pump, the
midpoint of 320--500, and 250\,EUR/kW for a hybrid in 2020 prices excluding
VAT, converted as above). The level follows Milieu Centraal, including VAT
and installation: 12\,000\,EUR for an 8\,kW electric heat pump in a terraced
house \citep{milieucentraalVolledigeWarmtepomp2026} and 8\,300\,EUR for a
4\,kW hybrid with a new boiler \citep{milieucentraalHybrideWarmtepomp2026},
The hybrid includes the boiler because the model treats it as one system with
one lifetime. $h_{fl}=1\,220$ hours gives the median terraced house in the
stock (9\,772\,kWh) the 8\,kW of the reference price. The CE Delft figures
bound the sensitivity range. Both heat pumps use one seasonal performance
factor for space heating and hot water together, because the model has a
single heat demand per dwelling, and the hybrid delivers 80\,\% of the heat
from its heat pump. Both heat pumps learn at 10\,\% per doubling of their
cumulative installations \citep{louwenREFLEXPolicyBrief2018}. In the
sensitivity analysis we vary this between zero, as observed for recent
installed costs in the United Kingdom
\citep{renaldiExperienceRatesLowcarbon2021}, and the 28\,\% found
historically for heat-pump prices \citep{weissReviewExperienceCurve2010a}.

Insulation costs and benefits in heat demand reduction are based on a table
with, for each dwelling type and building year category, insulation costs
from label x to y, calculated with a fixed and a variable per-m$^2$ part as
follows:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ic-ins}
  IC_{ins} = IC_{fixed} + IC_{var}\times A_{ins}
\end{equation}

Where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $IC_{ins}$ = Total insulation cost
  \item $IC_{fixed}$ = Fixed insulation cost
  \item $IC_{var}$ = Variable insulation cost per m$^2$
  \item $A_{ins}$ = Insulated area (m$^2$)
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Economic learning}

The new cost per unit after additional installations is calculated using the
learning curve formula (Wright's law) for each heating method option
(Equation~\ref{eq:learning}). Note, in this we assume that the learning rate
in the Netherlands specifically is similar to the global learning rate in
global markets, as we consider installed units on a national level.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:learning}
  C_n = C_{init}\times \left(\frac{Q_n}{Q_0}\right)^{\log_2(1 - LR)}
\end{equation}

Where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $C_n$ = New cost per unit
  \item $C_{init}$ = Initial cost per unit
  \item $Q_{init}$ = Initial number of units installed
  \item $Q_n$ = New total number of units installed ($Q_n = Q_0 + Q_{add}$)
  \item $LR$ = Learning rate (cost reduction per doubling of cumulative
  production)
\end{itemize}


\subsubsection{Subsidies, renovation and equipment lifetime}

Investment subsidies lower the up-front cost of heat pumps and heat-network
connections. They follow the Dutch schemes year by year. For owner-occupiers
and owner associations the national investment subsidy for sustainable energy
(ISDE), which for a typical hybrid heat pump was 2\,550 euros in 2022--2024
and 1\,925 euros in 2025, for an all-electric heat pump 3\,150 and 2\,825
euros, and for a heat-network connection 3\,325 euros until 2023 and 3\,775
euros from 2024 \citep{rvoISDE2025}. For private landlords the corresponding
scheme for rental dwellings. For housing associations a contribution to
heat-network connections only. After 2025 the amounts are held at their 2025
level. The calibration therefore sees the subsidies that applied over
2022--2024, and the scenarios vary them (\ref{app:scenario-input}).

The building stock is renovated approximately once every 25 years,
independently of heating decisions. These renovations are exogenous to the
model, but because they change the cost comparison, insulation levels and
heat distribution systems are improved at random in dwellings every 25 years.
Equipment does not fail deterministically. Each installed system's
end-of-life age is drawn from a normal distribution around its nominal
lifetime (standard deviation 1 year, limited to $\pm 3$ years), so that
replacements are spread out. A dwelling on district heating has no end of
life of its own, it stays connected unless a scenario lets it review its
heating.

\section{Calibration details}

\subsection{Calibration criterion}
\label{app:calibration}
A weight set is judged by the implausibility of each scored output $k$
(technology and year), the standard measure in history matching
\citep{vernonGalaxyFormationBayesian2010,
andrianakisBayesianHistoryMatching2015}:
\begin{equation}
  I_k = \frac{\lvert s_k - o_k \rvert}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\mathrm{MC},k}^2 +
  \sigma_{\mathrm{obs},k}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{disc}}^2}},
  \label{eq:implausibility}
\end{equation}
where $s_k$ is the simulated share and $o_k$ the observed share in percentage
points. The denominator collects the three reasons why a plausible weight set
would not match the observation exactly: the stochastic variation of the
model ($\sigma_{\mathrm{MC}}$, at most 0.06 percentage points), 
the uncertainty of the observation ($\sigma_{\mathrm{obs}} \approx 0.29$
percentage points). This mainly reflects the 1.8\,\% of dwellings whose
heating type is not allocated in the published neighborhood data. It is
estimated by allocating this share at random over the technologies, the
same way in every neighborhood, and by adding the rounding error of the
published shares, which contributes little. And third, the model
discrepancy ($\sigma_{\mathrm{disc}}$), an allowance for the model being a
simplified account of the heat transition, set to 0.5 percentage points. A
set is retained when the implausibility (Equation~\ref{eq:implausibility})
stays below 3 for every scored output, the conventional cutoff that follows
from the three-sigma rule \citep{pukelsheimThreeSigmaRule1994}. This admits
a deviation of about 1.7 percentage points on every scored output. Because
$\sigma_{\mathrm{disc}}$ and the cutoff are judgments,
Table~\ref{tab:retention_sensitivity} reports how many sets are retained when
either is varied.

Each weight set is run for two iterations. This is sufficient, because the
standard deviation between iterations, at most 0.06 percentage points, is
small compared with the deviation of 1.7 percentage points that the
criterion admits.

\subsection{Selecting the subsample}
Running every retained set through every scenario on a national scale is not
feasible, so the scenario analysis uses a subsample of 25 of the 124 retained
sets. Because the results are reported as the median and percentiles across
the sets, the subsample is chosen so that these statistics reproduce those of
the full retained ensemble. To this end, the baseline is run for every
retained set in Noord-Brabant, and the subsample is the combination of 25
sets whose 5, 25, 50, 75 and 95\,\% percentiles of the share of each
technology in 2030, 2040 and 2050 come closest to those of all retained sets
(the smallest sum of squared differences, standardized by the spread over the
retained sets, found by a local search with random restarts). The chosen sets
count equally. In the selection years, the subsample reproduces the medians
of the full ensemble within 1.1 and the 5 and 95\,\% percentiles within 1.3
percentage points. 
The bands in the scenario analysis are therefore the spread across the
plausible weight sets, with the median as a descriptive center, and not
probability intervals.

The sensitivity analysis takes its weight sets from the same subsample. The
Morris screening is repeated at four sets that span the two ways the retained
sets differ: the best-fitting set, the two ends of the cost--attitude line
(the most cost-driven and the most attitude-driven retained set), and the set
with the highest effective weight of the subjective norm. The Latin-hypercube
quantification is run at the single best-fitting set so that all single-set results refer
to one and the same point in the ensemble.

\section{Sensitivity assumptions}

\subsection{Parameters of the sensitivity analysis}
\label{app:sensitivity}
Table~\ref{tab:sens_params} lists the parameters screened with the Morris
method, with the range over which each is varied, its default value in the
scenario runs, and the basis of the range. The ranges of the cost parameters
are taken from the literature. Most behavioral and structural ranges are
modelling choices, because data on these aspects of the decision are lacking.
Two parameters that also shape the decision are not screened. The noise scale
of the choice $\lambda$ is calibrated together with the decision weights
(Section~\ref{sec:calibrating}), and the effort of switching enters the
decision only through its product with the calibrated effort weight, so that
varying it would count the same uncertainty twice. The review interval and
exit barrier of district heating are not screened either, because connected
dwellings stay connected in all scenarios.

The Morris method \citep{morris_factorial_1991,
campolongoEffectiveScreeningDesign2007} moves one parameter at a time along
random trajectories through the parameter space. Each step changes a single
parameter and records the resulting change in the outcome, an elementary
effect. The mean absolute elementary effect, $\mu^*$, ranks the parameters by
influence, the standard deviation of the elementary effects, $\sigma$,
indicates whether a parameter acts on its own or in combination with others.
The screening uses eight trajectories on a four-level grid for each of the
four screening weight sets (Section~\ref{sec:subsample}), in the baseline
scenario for Noord-Brabant over 2024--2050, with one iteration per run. Each
parameter is scaled to its range, so that $\mu^*$ is the change in a
technology's 2050 share, in percentage points, for a move across the full
range. The effects are averaged over the four sets, cost parameters weigh
more in the cost-driven sets, and attitude and norm parameters in the
attitude-driven sets.

The seven highest-ranked parameters (marked $\dagger$) are then varied
jointly in a Latin-hypercube sample of 80 combinations, each drawn uniformly
over the ranges in Table~\ref{tab:sens_params} and run with eight iterations,
at the best-fitting weight set, in the same scenario, region and period. All
other parameters are held at their defaults. 
For each technology's 2050 share we report the spread across the 80
combinations and, from a linear regression on the parameters, the change in
the share when a parameter moves across its range. The share of the
variation that this linear model explains (leave-one-out $R^2$), compared
with a model that adds squared and pairwise interaction terms, shows how far
the parameters act independently of each other.
